Mobility concepts for everyday work

Machines designed around livelihoods.

KUNA explores compact, practical mobility for people whose vehicle is also part of their work: simple architecture, low operating cost, modular utility, and a form that changes with the job.

PrinciplesUseful firstEvery component earns its place through daily use. Locally repairable. One platform, many businesses.

Problem statement the why

The people who move India's goods pay the most to do it. Fuel and upkeep eat a delivery rider's day, and the clean vehicles that could fix this cost too much to buy.

[0001] Cities

Choked streets

Indian cities rank among the worst in the world for air pollution and traffic jams. It is time to rethink mobility from the ground up.

[0002] Last mile

Slow last mile

Poor last-mile connectivity adds travel time for passengers and cargo alike.

[0003] Riders

Thin margins

A petrol two-wheeler burns 50–70 Rs a day in fuel and needs about 500 Rs a month in maintenance, which leaves delivery riders with hardly any income.

[0004] Businesses

Hiring overhead

Companies take on paperwork, licences and insurance for every rider they hire.

[0005] Adoption

EVs cost too much

Sustainable transport is sprouting, but the vehicles are too costly for mass adoption.

[0006] Street trade

No counter, no power

Juice sellers, ironing stalls and vegetable vendors need a surface to work on and electricity to run on, wherever they park.

The questionHow do we put a vehicle within reach of these workers that costs a tenth as much to run, needs no paperwork, and becomes their shop when it stops?

Vehicle 01KUNA Cargo E-Bike

(54)
Small electric cargo vehicle with removable side panels forming a workbench
(71)
Kuna Motors
(72)
Designer: Manoj Manduva
(57)

A low-cost electric cargo bike for last-mile delivery and street trade in Indian cities. A tubular truss frame carries a flat front deck, a long rear cargo deck, a hinged seat and a battery pack enclosed by two removable side panels. Lifted off, the panels lie across the rear carrier as a counter on a fold-out leg, and the battery powers a juicer, an iron or a lamp from an outlet on the pack.

(58)
Design reference: Honda Motra (1982) — flat front and rear carriers, a boxy pressed frame, fat knobby tyres.

Summary: the solution

[0007]

A cargo e-bike gives delivery riders and small business owners significant savings every day. No paperwork is needed if the top speed stays below 55 km/h.

[0008]

Because the total cost of ownership is low for EVs, businesses can adopt them faster. An EV usually costs 5–6 Rs a day to charge and needs relatively no maintenance. The cost to move goods drops by about 10×, a huge saving for small businesses and delivery riders. Only profits attract businesses.

[0009]

By focusing on the best product at the lowest price, we deliver value to the user and the buyer. The other benefits follow as a by-product.

Running costPetrol two-wheelerKUNA e-bike
Energy, per day50–70 Rs5–6 Rs
Maintenance, per month≈ 500 RsRelatively none
PaperworkLicence, registration, insuranceNone below 55 km/h

Workbench mode

[0010]

The two side panels that close the battery bay while riding come off with hand fasteners. Laid end to end on the rear carrier, they make a long counter. A leg folds down from the far end, the seat pivots forward on its hinge, and the centre stand holds the vehicle level. With the panels off, the outlet plate on the battery pack is in reach, so the stall runs on the same battery that moved it there (FIG. 7).

Juice stall

Juicer and dispenser on the counter, run from the outlet. A sun umbrella clamps to the leg.

FIG. 8

Iron pressing

A padded cover turns the counter into a pressing board. The steam iron plugs into the pack.

FIG. 9

Vegetable vendor

Inclined trays face the street. A mast carries a hanging scale and an evening lamp.

Vehicle 02KUNA EcoMule

(54)
Solar-assisted street-vendor vehicle with a swivel seat that turns toward customers
(71)
Kuna Motors
(72)
Designer: Manoj Manduva
(57)

EcoMule takes the compact, rugged utility logic of the original Mule and adapts it for an Indian street vendor: lighter, quieter, solar-assisted and designed to become a shop when it stops. A tubular chassis carries modular produce crates under a solar canopy. The driver seat swivels 180° and locks, so the vendor drives facing forward and sells facing customers without leaving the seat.

(58)
Design reference: the military Mule utility vehicle — compact footprint, exposed utility frame, large open cargo bed.

Description KUNA EcoMule

The core design principle

[0013]

Most commercial vehicles are designed around movement. A street-vendor vehicle spends much of its working day stationary, so EcoMule has to perform equally well as transport and as a place of work.

PrincipleDrive like a utility vehicle. Park like a storefront.

How EcoMule evolved

[0014]

Each iteration keeps one useful characteristic from the source vehicle and removes what does not belong in a low-cost neighbourhood retail vehicle.

Source

Rugged, compact and mechanically simple. Retained: compact footprint, exposed utility frame, large cargo area, functional simplicity.

FIG. 11

Iteration 01

Cargo is no longer only transported. It is displayed, weighed and sold directly from the vehicle.

FIG. 12

Iteration 02

The heavy military character gives way to a light urban frame, smaller wheels, open access and solar shade.

FIG. 13

Iteration 03

The vehicle changes posture when parked. The vendor does not leave the seat to start selling.

FIG. 14

Purpose-built for street retail

[0015]

EcoMule is a micro-enterprise platform rather than a miniature truck.

Solar canopy

Shade, plus power for lights, phone charging, digital payments and auxiliary battery charging.

Lightweight chassis

A simple tubular structure that cuts mass and can be built around locally serviceable parts.

Electric assist

Low-speed electric propulsion makes frequent neighbourhood stops easier and lowers daily running cost.

Modular crates

Produce stays visible and removable, which helps loading, sorting, cleaning and customer access.

Selling tools built in

Scale, price board, lighting and payment and phone positions are part of the vehicle.

Repairable construction

Minimal bodywork and accessible frame members keep routine repairs within reach of local workshops.

One seat, two working modes

[0016]

A 180° lockable swivel keeps one chair with the vendor all day. It faces forward for driving and turns toward customers once EcoMule is safely parked, without a second seat (FIG. 14, FIG. 15).

Digital selling, built in

[0017]

The point-of-sale console puts UPI payment, customer pricing, weighing and transaction information into the vendor's workspace (FIG. 16).

ElementWhat it does
UPI / QR paymentA clear scan-to-pay area for fast digital checkout.
Customer displayShows price, weight, offers and payment confirmation.
Digital weighingKeeps weighing and checkout in one customer-facing flow.
Vendor dashboardBattery, solar charging, sales totals and payment mix at a glance.

A platform for neighbourhood micro-businesses

[0018]

The same basic architecture could be adapted for fruit, flowers, tea, snacks, repair services, cold-box delivery, recycling collection and other mobile livelihoods.
